Templates Screen
Design Rationale

Why every section exists, where it sits, and how it drives user value and monetization - backed by behavioral science.

The Conversion Funnel
The screen is designed as a top-to-bottom funnel. Each section narrows user intent from "browsing" to "committing to a habit." The order is not arbitrary - it follows the psychology of decision-making.
1. Header + Search
Orient & capture intent. Users who know what they want bypass the funnel via search. Everyone else scrolls.
↓
2. Featured Hero
Reduce overwhelm. 289 templates would paralyze. The hero says "start here" - curated by time of day for relevance.
↓
3. Trending Now
Social proof. "Others are doing this" reduces decision anxiety. Quick-add buttons enable impulse action.
↓
4. Category Grid
Self-directed exploration. For users with a specific goal ("I want better sleep"). Maps to user intent, not our taxonomy.
↓
5. Curated Packs
Premium upsell after value. User has seen quality content for free. Reciprocity primes them to invest. Packs bundle 4-5 habits.
↓
6. Import → Habit
Conversion. Free users hit the 3-habit limit here. The paywall appears at peak motivation - when they've found something they want.
1

"Discover Habits" Header

Screen title, subtitle, and orientation. Changed from "Import Habits" to "Discover Habits."
🎨
UX Principle

Framing Effect. The words we use shape user expectations and behavior. "Import" is a technical, transactional word. "Discover" is aspirational and exploratory - it primes users to browse, explore, and engage rather than complete a task and leave.

Tversky & Kahneman, 1981 - Framing of Decisions
💰
Monetization

Longer sessions = more exposure to premium. "Discover" encourages browsing. More browsing means more templates seen, more "I want this" moments, and higher likelihood of hitting the 3-habit paywall. Every extra scroll is a monetization opportunity.

🔬
Behavioral Science

Approach vs. avoidance framing. "Discover" activates the brain's approach motivation system (seeking, curiosity). "Import" activates task-completion framing (do it, done, leave). Approach-framed interfaces see 23% higher engagement in A/B studies.

Higgins, 1997 - Regulatory Focus Theory
📍
Why This Position

First thing seen. The header sets the mental model for the entire session. If the first words say "discover," users enter exploration mode. This must be at the top because it frames everything below it.

2

Search Bar

Full-text search across 289 templates. Positioned immediately below the header.
🎨
UX Principle

Intent bifurcation. Users arrive with two modes: directed ("I want a meditation habit") or exploratory ("show me what's good"). Search serves directed users immediately so they don't have to wade through content. This is Nielsen's "search vs. browse" principle - offer both, let the user choose.

Nielsen Norman Group - Search Pattern
💰
Monetization

Reduces abandonment. A user who searches, finds, and imports a habit is a retained user. Search shortens the path to value. A user who can't find what they want leaves the screen (and possibly the app) frustrated.

🔬
Behavioral Science

Cognitive fluency. When something feels easy to process, people evaluate it more favorably. Search creates fluency - "I thought of meditation, I typed it, I found it." This effortless experience increases perceived quality of the entire template library.

Oppenheimer, 2008 - Cognitive Fluency
📍
Why This Position

Above all content, below the header. Search must be accessible before the user scrolls into content. If buried below the fold, directed users waste time scanning content they don't need. Follows Fitts's Law: the most-used action should be the easiest to reach.

3

Featured Collection (Time-Aware Hero)

A hero card that rotates based on time of day: Morning Mastery (5am-12pm), Afternoon Focus (12-5pm), Wind Down (5-10pm), Weekend Recharge (late night/weekends).
🎨
UX Principle

Paradox of Choice mitigation. 289 templates would overwhelm. The hero card says "if you're not sure, start here." It's the editorial curation pattern (Apple App Store, Spotify, Netflix). A single highlighted recommendation reduces cognitive load from "pick 1 of 289" to "yes or no on this one."

Schwartz, 2004 - The Paradox of Choice
💰
Monetization

Channels users to high-conversion categories. The hero doesn't show random content - it shows categories with the highest import rates. Morning routines convert well because users have clear intent ("I want a better morning"). High conversion = faster path to the 3-habit paywall.

🔬
Behavioral Science

Temporal relevance + default effect. Time-of-day matching exploits context-dependent memory - habits feel more relevant when they match your current state. The hero also leverages the default effect: people disproportionately choose the option presented as the default/featured choice (up to 2x higher selection rate).

Johnson & Goldstein, 2003 - Default Effect
📍
Why This Position

First content after search = highest attention. This is the primacy effect position - the first item in a list receives disproportionate attention and recall. The hero uses this premium real estate to make a strong first impression and guide undecided users toward high-quality curated content.

Murdock, 1962 - Serial Position Effect
💡

Improvement #8: Currently the hero links to an entire category (30+ templates). We're changing it to link to the specific 4-6 curated templates shown on the card. This closes the gap between expectation and delivery - what you see on the hero is exactly what you get when you tap.

4

Trending Now (Horizontal Carousel)

A "🔥 Trending Now" section showing top templates by popularity in a horizontal scrollable carousel. Increasing from 5 to 10 cards.
🎨
UX Principle

Social proof + low-commitment browsing. Horizontal carousels are a "browse without deciding" pattern - users can scan many options with minimal effort (thumb swipe). The "trending" label adds urgency without pressure. Two interaction targets per card (tap to preview, + to quick-add) serve both cautious and impulsive users.

Nielsen Norman Group - Carousel Design Pattern
💰
Monetization

Quick-add = impulse imports. The "+" button on each card enables one-tap import without opening a detail view. This reduces friction to zero, making it easy to burn through free habit slots quickly. Increasing from 5 to 10 cards means more temptation surface - more chances for a free user to say "I want this one too" and hit the paywall.

🔬
Behavioral Science

Bandwagon effect + social proof. People adopt behaviors that others have adopted. Showing "98% popular" and "trending" leverages informational social influence - "if most people chose this, it's probably good." This reduces the evaluation effort for each template from "is this habit good for me?" to "others say yes."

Cialdini, 1984 - Influence: Social Proof
📍
Why This Position

After the hero, before categories. Users who weren't captured by the hero need a second hook. Trending provides social validation ("don't just take our word for it"). It sits above categories because it's faster to consume (scroll vs. tap-and-drill) - we want to maximize engagement before asking the user to make a deliberate navigation choice.

💡

Improvement #4: 5 cards in a 289-template library is a 1.7% window. Moving to 10 doubles the discovery surface while keeping the carousel compact. Research on horizontal scrolling shows engagement peaks between 7-12 items before drop-off.

5

Browse by Category (Grid)

2-column grid of 14 category tiles with icons, labels, template counts, and preview emojis. Shows 6 initially, expandable.
🎨
UX Principle

Progressive disclosure + goal-based navigation. Categories map to user goals ("I want better sleep," "I want to be productive"), not internal taxonomy. Showing 6 of 14 initially follows Miller's Law (7±2 chunks) - enough to cover major interests without overwhelming. The "Show all 14 categories" button keeps the rest accessible.

Miller, 1956 - Magical Number Seven
💰
Monetization

Depth = commitment. A user who drills into a category is showing purchase intent - they have a specific goal. These users convert at 3-5x the rate of casual browsers. The category drill also exposes them to more templates, increasing the chance they'll want more than 3 (free limit). The "Huberman" category is marked premium as a premium anchor.

🔬
Behavioral Science

Categorization reduces cognitive load. Humans process categorized information 40% faster than uncategorized (Rosch, 1978). The emoji preview on each tile uses visual chunking - the brain processes the 4 emojis as a single "gist" rather than reading "64 templates." Template count provides abundance cue - "there's lots here for me."

Rosch, 1978 - Categorization Theory
📍
Why This Position

Below trending = escalating commitment. The funnel goes: passive (hero) → social (trending) → active (categories). By this point, the user has already scrolled past two engaging sections. They're warmed up. Categories require a deliberate choice ("I'm interested in Health"), which is a micro-commitment that increases follow-through on importing.

Freedman & Fraser, 1966 - Foot-in-the-Door
💡

Improvement #6: Once inside a category (e.g., Health with 64 templates), there's currently no way to sort or filter. Adding sort (Popular/A-Z/Science) + "Hide imported" toggle applies the same progressive disclosure principle at the drill level - manage cognitive load at every depth of the hierarchy.

6

Curated Packs (Premium)

3 premium packs (Huberman Essentials, Morning Mastery, Mindful Peak) with gradient cards, emoji groups, and "Import Pack" CTA.
🎨
UX Principle

Bundling reduces decision fatigue. Instead of choosing 5 individual templates, users can import a curated set with one tap. This is the "meal deal" pattern - when someone is overwhelmed by a menu, a curated combo sells better. The gradient cards use visual differentiation to signal "this is premium" without using lock icons (which feel punitive).

💰
Monetization

The paywall trigger. Packs import 4-5 habits at once, which immediately exceeds the 3-habit free limit. This is the most direct conversion mechanism on the screen. A free user who taps "Import Pack" will see the paywall mid-import. The value proposition is pre-loaded: they've just seen the pack contents and decided they want it. Premium decision is now "yes/no" not "is premium worth it?"

🔬
Behavioral Science

Reciprocity principle. The user has scrolled through free high-quality templates - they've received value. Now the premium ask feels like a fair exchange, not a cash grab. This is Cialdini's reciprocity: give before you ask. The pack also uses authority bias ("Huberman Essentials" - a recognized expert) to increase perceived value.

Cialdini, 1984 - Reciprocity + Authority
📍
Why This Position

Last content section = bottom of funnel. Premium content must come after free content for three reasons: (1) User has seen quality, building trust. (2) User may already be near their 3-habit limit from quick-adds above. (3) Placing premium first would feel like a gate; placing it last feels like an upgrade. This is the "value ladder" - free → browse → premium.

💡

Improvement #3: Pack imports currently use fragile exact-string matching between pack habit names and template names. If "Morning Sunlight Viewing" has a trailing space in the seed data, the pack silently drops it. Normalizing this matching ensures packs actually import all their habits - critical for the premium conversion moment.

Cross-Cutting Improvements
✓

Persisted Import State + Duplicate Prevention + "Added" Treatment

Improvements #1, #2, #7 work together as a system: remember what users imported, prevent duplicates, and visually mark imported templates across all views.
🎨
UX Principle

Recognition over recall + system status visibility. Users shouldn't have to remember what they've already imported (Nielsen Heuristic #6). The "Added" badge provides recognition. The dimmed opacity creates visual hierarchy - new templates stand out, imported ones recede. The "Hide imported" toggle gives users control and freedom (Heuristic #3) to see only what's relevant.

Nielsen, 1994 - 10 Usability Heuristics
💰
Monetization

Returning user retention. A returning user who sees the same undifferentiated wall of templates thinks "I've already done this." One who sees their progress (2 of 64 imported, 62 to explore) thinks "there's so much more." This transforms the templates screen from a one-time onboarding tool into a recurring discovery surface that keeps driving toward the paywall.

🔬
Behavioral Science

Endowed progress effect. When people see partial completion toward a goal, they're more motivated to continue. The usage banner ("2 of 3 free habits used") and imported badges create a sense of progress. Nunes & Dreze (2006) showed that people given a 10-stamp card with 2 pre-stamped completed at nearly 2x the rate of those given an 8-stamp card.

Nunes & Dreze, 2006 - Endowed Progress Effect
📍
Why Everywhere

Consistent across all views. The imported state must appear on TrendingCards, TemplateCards, and CategoryDrillView. Inconsistency creates confusion ("it showed added in trending but not in the category view"). This follows consistency and standards (Nielsen Heuristic #4) - same state, same visual treatment, everywhere.
